TRAINING REFERENCE MANUAL 
Harmonious Coexistence with Elephants in Namibia
SYNOPSIS
This training reference manual is designed to educate communities living in Namibia's rural 
areas on elephant behavior, incorporating both traditional indigenous knowledge and 
practical strategies for managing human-elephant interactions. By promoting a deeper 
understanding of elephant behavior and ecological roles, the manual seeks to foster 
harmonious coexistence between humans and these valuable animals.
The manual serves as a comprehensive guide for communal farmers, commercial farmers, 
community game guards, and safari guides, providing them with the necessary knowledge 
and tools to mitigate potential conflict, safeguard livelihoods, and protect wildlife. Through a 
combination of modern conservation techniques and indigenous practices, the aim is to 
empower local communities with sustainable solutions that enhance both human and 
elephant well-being. 


Introduction
Namibia, with its vast landscapes and rich biodiversity, is home to a significant population of
elephants. These majestic creatures, while being a national treasure and a cornerstone of 
tourism, often come into conflict with human activities. As human populations expand and 
encroach upon elephant habitats, the instances of human-elephant conflict (HEC) have 
increased, posing a significant challenge to both conservation efforts and the livelihoods of 
local communities.
The purpose of this training manual is to provide comprehensive guidelines and practical 
strategies for mitigating human-elephant conflict in Namibia. This manual is designed for 
conservationists, local authorities, community members, and anyone involved in wildlife 
management and rural development. Our goal is to foster coexistence between humans and 
elephants by promoting understanding, implementing effective conflict mitigation 
techniques, and encouraging sustainable practices.
Human-elephant conflict in Namibia manifests in various forms, including crop destruction, 
property damage, and occasional human casualties. These conflicts not only threaten the 
safety and economic stability of local communities but also endanger the elephant 
population, which may face retaliatory killings and habitat fragmentation as a result. 
Addressing HEC is crucial for:
1. Conservation of Biodiversity: Elephants play a vital role in maintaining the ecological 
balance. Protecting them ensures the health of entire ecosystems.
2. Economic Stability: Many communities rely on agriculture for their livelihoods. 
Mitigating conflict helps protect crops and property, ensuring economic stability.
3. Community Safety and Well-being: Reducing the risk of encounters with elephants 
improves the quality of life for people living in affected areas.
4. Sustainable Development: Harmonious coexistence between humans and wildlife is 
essential for sustainable development and the preservation of natural resources.
Structure of the Manual
This manual is organized into several key sections, each addressing a different aspect of 
human-elephant conflict mitigation:
1. Understanding Elephants: Insights into elephant behavior, including communication 
methods, social dynamics, and reproductive behaviors.
2. Elephant Communication and Social Dynamics: Insights into interactions between 
elephants, from infrasound communication to maternal care and mating rituals.
3. Understanding Desert-Dwelling Elephants: Examination of desert-dwelling elephants 
and their adaptations to arid environments, migration patterns, and physical 
characteristics.
4. Indigenous Knowledge and Traditions: Exploration of the rich history of human elephant interactions in Namibia and of the wisdom of indigenous communities.

5. Conservation and Sustainable Management: Discovering conservation challenges for 
elephant populations in Namibia and the efforts made to protect them.
By understanding the complexities of human-elephant conflict and implementing effective 
mitigation strategies, we can work towards a future where both humans and elephants 
thrive. This manual aims to equip you with the knowledge and tools necessary to address 
HEC in Namibia, fostering a harmonious relationship between people and these magnificent 
animals. Together, we can ensure the continued survival of Namibia’s elephants and the 
prosperity of its communities.

Unit 1: Understanding Elephant Behavior
Lesson 1: Elephant History
Elephant Presence
Elephants have roamed the landscapes of Namibia for thousands of years, playing a crucial 
role in shaping the country's diverse ecosystems. Historically, Namibia's elephants were 
found across the northern regions, from the savannas of Etosha National Park to the rugged 
terrains of Kunene and the Caprivi Strip. These majestic animals are primarily African 
savanna elephants, the largest land mammals on earth, which are known for their 
impressive intelligence, complex social structures, and significant ecological impact (WWF 
Namibia, n.d.). The African savanna elephant (Loxodonta africana) is endangered with a 
globally declining population trend (Gobush et al. 2022). For Namibia, elephant populations 
have been recovering, with the most recent national estimate of 24,000 elephants possibly 
being an underestimate given expanding ranges and the exclusion of elephants in private 
game reserves in the national population numbers (MEFT, 2021; Thomson, 2021). Elephants 
can be found in the northern parts of Namibia, specifically in the northwest, in Etosha 
National Park, in the Kavango West Region and Mangetti area, in Khaudum National Park 
and Nyae Nyae Conservancy, and in the Zambezi Region and Bwabwata National Park. 
Significant populations can be found in protected areas like Etosha National Park, Bwabwata 
National Park, and the Kunene Region (MEFT, 2021).
Impact of Historical Events
The presence of elephants in Namibia was influenced by historical events.
During the early 20th century, Namibia, then known as South West Africa, was under 
German colonial rule, followed by South African administration after World War I. During 
this period, elephants, like many other wildlife species, were subjected to unregulated 
hunting and habitat encroachment. The demand for ivory led to significant poaching, 
reducing elephant numbers drastically. The establishment of protected areas, such as Etosha 
National Park in 1907, marked the beginning of formal conservation efforts, although 
enforcement was initially weak (Leggett, 2006). Namibia gained independence in 1990, a 
pivotal moment for wildlife conservation in the country. However, the preceding decades 
were tumultuous for elephants due to the Angolan Civil War (1975-2002). The conflict spilled 
over into northern Namibia, particularly affecting the Kunene and Kavango regions. During 
this period, poaching for ivory was rampant, driven by the need to fund military operations. 
The war also displaced local communities, leading to increased human-elephant conflicts as 
elephants ventured into areas they previously avoided (Martin, 2005). The preceding 
Angolan War of Independence had already caused a poaching crisis that led to a dramatic 
decrease in elephant numbers. By the end of the war in 1974, the elephant population in 
Angola was severely depleted. This decline had repercussions in neighboring Namibia, as 
poachers increasingly crossed into Namibian territory to continue their activities. The cross border poaching highlighted the broader regional impact of the war on wildlife conservation 
(Brito et al., 2011). Elephant populations in the Zambezi Region and other parts of the 
country were heavily targeted. Poaching networks were sophisticated, often involving 
organized crime syndicates. This period saw some of the lowest elephant numbers in 
4
Namibia’s history. Conservation efforts were minimal, and anti-poaching measures were 
inadequate, exacerbating the crisis (Douglas-Hamilton, 1972). The introduction of 
community-based natural resource management (CBNRM) in the 1990s was a turning point 
for elephant conservation in Namibia. This approach empowered local communities to 
manage and benefit from wildlife, including elephants. The establishment of conservancies 
allowed communities to derive income from eco-tourism and trophy hunting, incentivizing 
the protection of elephants. As a result, poaching incidents decreased significantly, and 
elephant populations began to recover (Hoole & Berkes, 2010). Today, Namibia's elephant 
populations are on a positive trajectory, thanks to robust conservation policies and the 
active involvement of local communities. However, challenges remain. Habitat loss due to 
agricultural expansion, human-elephant conflicts, and the ongoing threat of poaching still 
pose significant risks. Climate change is also emerging as a critical factor, with changing 
rainfall patterns and prolonged droughts affecting elephant habitats and food availability 
(MEFT, 2019).
Cultural Importance
Elephants hold a profound cultural significance for various ethnic groups in Namibia, 
embodying symbols of wisdom, strength, and ancestral connections. These majestic animals 
are woven into the fabric of traditional beliefs, rituals, and societal values, reflecting the 
deep bond between people and wildlife. This is explored further in Unit 1, Lesson 4.
Conservation Efforts
In response to the threats posed by poaching and habitat loss, Namibia has implemented 
several conservation initiatives aimed at protecting its elephant populations. Key efforts 
include:
1. Community-Based Natural Resource Management (CBNRM): 
• Namibia pioneered the CBNRM program, which empowers local communities to 
manage and benefit from wildlife resources. This approach involves creating 
conservancies where communities can engage in sustainable tourism and trophy 
hunting, with revenues supporting local development and conservation initiatives 
(Hoole & Berkes, 2010).
• Over 86 communal conservancies have been established, covering nearly 20% of 
Namibia's land area, providing a haven for elephants and other wildlife (WWF 
Namibia, n.d.).
2. Anti-Poaching Measures: 
• The Namibian government, in collaboration with non-governmental organizations 
(NGOs) and international partners, has implemented robust anti-poaching measures. 
These include training and equipping ranger units, deploying anti-poaching patrols, 
and using surveillance technologies such as drones and GPS tracking (Namibia 
Ministry of Environment and Tourism, 2019).
5
• The establishment of the Wildlife Protection Services has enhanced law enforcement 
capabilities, leading to significant reductions in poaching incidents (Chase & Griffin, 
2011).
3. Transboundary Conservation Areas (TFCAs): 
• Namibia is a key participant in the Kavango-Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area 
(KAZA TFCA), one of the world's largest transboundary conservation initiatives. KAZA 
TFCA spans five countries: Angola, Botswana, Namibia, Zambia, and Zimbabwe, 
creating a vast landscape for elephant migration and habitat connectivity (Chase & 
Griffin, 2011).
• This collaboration aims to facilitate the free movement of elephants across borders, 
reduce human-elephant conflicts, and promote regional biodiversity conservation 
(WWF Namibia, n.d.).
4. Protected Areas and National Parks: 
• Namibia has designated several national parks and protected areas that serve as 
critical habitats for elephants. Key protected areas include Etosha National Park, 
Bwabwata National Park, and the Kunene Region. These areas provide safe havens 
for elephants, ensuring they have access to necessary resources such as water and 
food (Leggett, 2006).
• Management plans for these parks include measures to mitigate human-elephant 
conflicts, maintain ecological balance, and promote sustainable tourism (MEFT, 
2019).

Lesson 2: Elephant Features
The African savanna elephant (Loxodonta africana), the largest land mammal on Earth, is a 
striking and iconic species with several distinct physical features. Here are some key 
characteristics:
Size and Weight
• Height: 
o Adult male savanna elephants typically stand between 3 to 4 meters tall at 
the shoulder, on average 3.3 meters 
o Adult females are slightly smaller, standing about 2.5 to 3 meters tall, on 
average 2.7 meters
• Weight: 
o Adult males can weigh between 4,500 to 6,800 kilograms
o Adult females generally weigh less, between 2,700 to 3,600 kilograms.
Physical Features
• Tusks: 
o Both male and female African savanna elephants have tusks, which are 
elongated incisor teeth.
o Tusks can grow up to 3 meters in length and weigh over 45 kg.
o They are used for digging, stripping bark from trees, and as weapons in 
conflicts.
• Ears: 
o African savanna elephants have large ears that can reach up to 2 meters (6.6 
feet) in length.
o The ears are used for thermoregulation, as flapping them helps to cool the 
blood.
• Trunk: 
o The trunk is a versatile, elongated fusion of the nose and upper lip, containing 
over 40,000 muscles.
o It is used for breathing, smelling, touching, grasping, and producing sound.
o The trunk can weigh up to 140 kg and hold up to 12 liters of water.
• Skin: 
o The skin of an African savanna elephant is gray and thick, about 2.5 cm in 
most places.
o It has a rough texture with deep wrinkles, which help retain moisture and 
protect from parasites.
• Feet: 
o Elephants have large, padded feet that help distribute their massive weight.
o African savanna elephants have four toenails on their front feet and three on 
their back feet

Identification of Individual Elephants
Identifying individual elephants can be challenging but is crucial for research, conservation, 
and managing human-elephant conflict. Here are some methods and characteristics used for 
identification:
Physical Markers
1. Tusks: 
• Shape, size, and curvature of tusks are unique to each elephant.
• Some elephants may have broken or missing tusks, providing distinct 
identification markers.
2. Ears: 
• The shape and size of the ears can vary, with unique patterns of veins visible 
on the back of the ears.
• Notches, tears, and holes in the ears can serve as identifying features.
3. Trunk: 
• The trunk may have distinctive scars or pigmentation patterns.
• Some elephants might have a particular way they hold or use their trunk, 
adding to their individual identification.
4. Skin: 
• Scars, wrinkles, and pigmentation patterns on the skin can help distinguish 
individuals.
• The texture and location of these features are often unique.
5. Tail: 
• The length, shape, and overall condition of an elephant’s tail can significantly 
vary between individuals. 
• Tail hair (number, thickness, color) can also be used to identify individual 
elephants.
6. Footprints:
• Footprint size and shape, including the pattern of cracks and lines on the sole 
of the feet, are unique to each elephant. By measuring the circumference of 
the footprint, the height of the individual elephant can be estimated.
Behavioral Traits
1. Movement Patterns: 
• Individual elephants may have specific walking patterns or speeds.
• They might frequent particular routes or areas more than others.
8
2. Social Interactions: 
• Behavior in social contexts, such as dominance hierarchies, affiliative 
behaviors, and interactions with specific individuals, can help identify them.
• Unique vocalizations and communication methods are also significant.
3. Feeding Habits: 
• Preferences for certain types of vegetation or feeding techniques can be 
distinctive.
• Some elephants may use their tusks in specific ways to strip bark or dig for 
roots.
In conclusion, the African savanna elephant's distinctive physical characteristics and 
individual markers make them fascinating subjects for study and conservation. Accurate 
identification of individuals is crucial for monitoring their populations, understanding their 
behavior, and implementing effective conservation strategies.
Lesson 3: Age and Ecology of Elephants
Lifespan
African savanna elephants (Loxodonta africana) have relatively long lifespans. In the wild, 
they typically live between 60 to 70 years, while only averaging around 40 to 50 years in 
captivity. Lifespans can be influenced by several factors, including environmental conditions, 
availability of food and water, and human impacts such as poaching and habitat destruction
(Moss, 2001; Wittemyer, 2011; Clubb & Mason, 2002). 
Diet
Elephants are herbivores and have a diverse diet that varies with the seasons and their 
environment. Their diet includes:
• Grasses: 
During the wet season, grasses form the bulk of their diet. Fresh, green grasses 
provide high nutritional value and are readily available in the savanna (Owen-Smith, 
1988).
• Leaves, Shoots, and Bark:
In the dry season, elephants shift to eating more woody vegetation. Leaves, shoots, 
and bark from trees and shrubs become primary food sources. Bark is particularly 
important as it provides essential nutrients like calcium and fiber (Owen-Smith, 
1988).
• Fruits and Seeds: 
Elephants consume various fruits and seeds when they are available. These are rich 
in vitamins and minerals. For example, elephants are known to seek out the fruits of 
the marula tree (Shannon et al., 2006). 
9
• Roots and Tubers: 
In times of food scarcity, elephants dig for roots and tubers, which are valuable for 
their water and nutrient content. This ability to dig for subterranean food resources 
helps them survive in arid conditions (Owen-Smith, 1988).
Elephants require large quantities of food daily, consuming between 150 to 300 kilograms of 
vegetation daily to meet their energy needs and maintain their body weight. As elephants 
only digest 40 percent of their food, their dung contains undigested plants and seeds with 
valuable nutrients for other animals to eat (EHRA, 2022).
They also need to drink water regularly and can consume up to 200 liters per day (Moss, 
1988). In addition to their natural diet, elephants will also include foods into their diet that 
humans feed them. This can lead to dangerous encounters, and is further touched upon in 
Unit 3, Lesson 2.
Feeding Behavior
• Foraging Patterns:
Elephants are both grazers and browsers, and their diet reflects the availability of 
different food resources throughout the year. They exhibit selective feeding 
behaviors, often preferring certain plant species (Owen-Smith, 1988).
• Impact on Ecosystem:
Elephants play a significant role in their ecosystems through their feeding habits. By 
breaking branches, uprooting trees, and trampling vegetation, they help shape the 
landscape, which promotes biodiversity and maintains the balance of plant species
(Owen-Smith, 1988). Their partly undigested dung serves as food for other wildlife 
and farm animals, which has been recognized by farmers in Namibia; elephant dung 
is fed to livestock (Munembome, 2024).
Migration Patterns and Movement Behavior
Elephants are known for their extensive and complex movement patterns. Their movements 
are influenced by various factors, including the availability of food and water, social 
structure, and environmental conditions. Key aspects of their migration and movement 
behavior include:
• Seasonal Migrations:
o African savanna elephants often migrate seasonally in response to the 
availability of water and food. During the wet season, they disperse over 
larger areas as water sources and vegetation are abundant. In the dry season, 
they congregate around permanent water sources (Loarie et al., 2009).
o This seasonal migration helps elephants optimize their foraging efficiency and 
access essential resources throughout the year (Foley et al., 2008).
• Home Ranges: 
o The size of an elephant's home range can vary significantly depending on the 
availability of resources, habitat type, and social structure. Home ranges can 
span from a few hundred to several thousand square kilometers (Galanti et 
al., 2006).
10
o Within their home ranges, elephants use specific areas more intensively, such 
as feeding sites, water sources, and social gathering points. These preferred 
areas often change with the seasons and resource availability (Loarie et al., 
2009).
• Social Movements: Elephant herds, primarily composed of related females and their 
offspring, move together under the leadership of a matriarch. Bulls (males) may 
travel alone or form temporary bachelor groups, particularly younger males (Poole & 
Moss, 2008).
• Corridors: Elephants use specific pathways, known as corridors, to move between 
key areas such as feeding grounds, water sources, and breeding sites. These corridors 
are crucial for maintaining genetic diversity and ecological balance (Douglas-Hamilton 
et al., 2005).
Environmental Factors Influencing Elephant Ecology
Several environmental factors play a significant role in shaping the ecology of the African 
savanna elephant:
• Climate: 
o Seasonal variations: Elephants inhabit regions with distinct wet and dry 
seasons, which greatly affect their movement patterns, foraging behavior, 
and water needs. During the wet season, elephants have access to abundant 
food and water resources, leading to dispersed movement. In contrast, the 
dry season forces elephants to congregate around permanent water sources 
and adapt their diet to available resources (Owen-Smith, 1988).
o Temperature: Extreme temperatures can impact elephant behavior and 
physiology. Elephants tend to seek shade and water to cool off during hot 
days, and their large ears aid in thermoregulation by dissipating heat (Poole & 
Granli, 2008).
• Vegetation: 
o Food availability: Elephants are generalist herbivores and their diet includes 
grasses, leaves, bark, fruits, and roots. The availability and type of vegetation 
vary seasonally and regionally, influencing their foraging patterns. For 
instance, they consume more grasses during the wet season and switch to 
browsing woody plants in the dry season (Codron et al., 2006).
o Habitat Types: Elephants inhabit various habitats including savannas, 
woodlands, and forests. Each habitat offers different types of food and 
shelter. Savannas provide ample grass and space for movement, while 
woodlands and forests offer diverse browsing opportunities and cover (Owen Smith, 1988).
• Water Availability: 
11
o Access to Water Sources: Water is a critical resource for elephants, 
influencing their daily movements and seasonal migrations. Elephants need to 
drink regularly, and their distribution is closely tied to the availability of 
water. In arid regions, elephants are known to dig for water in dry riverbeds, 
demonstrating their ability to adapt to scarce water conditions (Loarie et al., 
2009).
o Seasonal Water Bodies: Temporary water bodies formed during the rainy 
season provide essential hydration and cooling for elephants. As these water 
sources dry up, elephants migrate to more permanent water bodies, 
sometimes covering long distances (Foley et al., 2008).
• Human Activities: 
o Habitat Fragmentation: Human activities such as agriculture, urbanization, 
and infrastructure development have led to habitat fragmentation, which 
restricts elephant movements and access to essential resources. This 
fragmentation also increases human-elephant conflicts (Douglas-Hamilton et 
al., 2005).
o Poaching and Conservation: Poaching for ivory has historically been a 
significant threat to elephant populations, leading to skewed sex ratios and 
social disruptions. Conservation efforts, including anti-poaching measures and 
protected areas, aim to mitigate these threats and support population 
recovery (Thouless et al., 2016).
In summary, the ecology of African savanna elephants is shaped by their dietary needs, 
migratory behaviors, social structures, and various environmental factors. Understanding 
these aspects is crucial for developing effective conservation strategies to ensure the long term survival of elephant populations and the ecosystems they inhabit.
Lesson 4: Purpose and Meaning of Elephants
Cultural Significance in Namibian Communities
Elephants hold profound cultural significance in many Namibian communities. For the Himba 
people, who reside primarily in the Kunene Region of northwestern Namibia, elephants are 
revered as sacred animals. The Himba believe that elephants are the spirits of their 
ancestors, who continue to guide and protect the living. This belief is deeply rooted in their 
cosmology, where ancestral spirits play a crucial role in everyday life and the well-being of 
the community. The Himba people often incorporate elephants into their rituals and oral 
traditions, viewing them as guardians of the land and symbols of endurance, resilience, and 
health (Suzman, 2001).
The San communities, also known as Bushmen, have a rich cultural history intertwined with 
the natural world. Elephants feature prominently in their folklore and spiritual practices. The 
12
San believe that elephants possess a unique spiritual power and intelligence that surpasses 
that of other animals. They often tell stories that depict elephants as wise beings that can 
communicate with humans and other animals. In their traditional healing practices, the San 
may use parts of the elephant, such as the bones or dung, for medicinal purposes, believing 
that these carry the animal's potent spirit and healing properties (Harries-Jones, 2004).
In northern Namibia, the Owambo people hold elephants in high esteem, associating them 
with rain and fertility. Owambo mythology often includes elephants as harbingers of rain, 
essential for agriculture and the sustenance of their communities. During times of drought, 
some Owambo tribes perform rituals to invoke the spirits of elephants, hoping to bring rain 
and prosperity. Elephants are also seen as symbols of authority and power, often depicted in 
traditional art and crafts that celebrate the majesty and significance of these animals in their 
culture (Suzman, 2001).
The Herero people, who live in various parts of Namibia, including the central and eastern 
regions, regard elephants as symbols of leadership and strength. Elephants are often 
featured in Herero folklore as noble creatures that embody the qualities of a good leader: 
wisdom, patience, and the ability to navigate difficult terrains. The Herero have traditional 
songs and dances that celebrate the elephant's role in their culture, and these performances 
are an integral part of community gatherings and festivals (Hoole & Berkes, 2010).
The Damara people of Namibia hold elephants in high cultural regard, seeing them as 
symbols of strength, wisdom, as spiritual beings and messengers connected to their 
ancestors. Elephants are deeply embedded in their traditional beliefs and are viewed as part 
of the natural order that must be respected and coexisted with. The Damara's traditional 
knowledge, which includes understanding elephant behavior and migration patterns, plays a 
significant role in maintaining this harmonious relationship. Such respect for elephants is 
reflected in their sustainable land management practices, ensuring minimal conflict with 
these majestic creatures (Sullivan, 1998).
Economic Value in Tourism
Elephants are a cornerstone of Namibia's wildlife tourism industry, which is a significant 
contributor to the national economy.
• Safari & Ecotourism: 
Elephants are a major draw for safari enthusiasts, providing a unique wildlife viewing 
experience. The presence of elephants enhances the appeal of national parks and 
private reserves, leading to increased visitation and tourism revenue. According to 
the Namibia Tourism Board, wildlife tourism, including elephant sightings, is a key 
component of the country's tourism sector, which generated approximately USD 500 
million in 2019 (Namibia Tourism Board, 2019).
• Employment: 
The tourism industry provides numerous jobs for local communities, including roles 
as guides, rangers, and hospitality staff. The presence of elephants and other wildlife 
supports a wide range of employment opportunities, contributing to local economies 
and improving livelihoods (Barnes et al., 2002).
13
• Conservation Incentives:
Revenue generated from elephant-based tourism supports conservation efforts, 
including anti-poaching initiatives, habitat restoration, and research programs. By 
linking economic benefits to conservation, there is a greater incentive to protect 
elephant populations and their habitats (Thouless et al., 2016).
Role in Maintaining Ecosystem Balance
Elephants play a crucial role in maintaining the balance and health of their ecosystems.
• Habitat Modification: 
o Elephants influence vegetation structure by feeding on trees and bushes. 
They break branches, uproot small trees, and strip bark, which helps maintain 
open savanna landscapes. This activity prevents woody plant encroachment, 
allowing grasslands to thrive and supporting a diversity of herbivores (Scholes 
& Archer, 1997).
o Their foraging behavior can create a mosaic of different vegetation types, 
which increases habitat heterogeneity and promotes biodiversity (Owen Smith, 1988).
• Seed Dispersal: 
o Elephants are effective seed dispersers. They consume large quantities of 
fruits and vegetables, and their digestive system aids in the scarification of 
seeds, enhancing germination rates. Seeds are deposited in nutrient-rich 
dung, providing an ideal environment for growth (Dudley, 1999).
o By dispersing seeds over large distances, elephants help regenerate plant 
populations and maintain genetic diversity within plant species (Blake et al., 
2009).
• Water Access: 
o Elephants are known to dig for water in dry riverbeds and create water holes 
that can be used by other animals. These water sources are critical during dry 
seasons, providing hydration for a variety of species (Western, 1989).
o Their movement between water sources also creates pathways that other 
animals can follow, enhancing access to water across the landscape (Douglas Hamilton et al., 2005).
• Soil Aeration and Fertilization:
o As elephants forage and move across their habitat, they trample the ground, 
which helps aerate the soil. This activity improves soil health and promotes 
plant growth.
o Their dung is rich in nutrients and acts as a natural fertilizer, enriching the 
soil and supporting plant communities. Dung beetles and other 
decomposers further break down the dung, contributing to nutrient cycling 
(Fowler & Cope, 1982).
• Supporting Other Wildlife:
o The physical alterations elephants make to their environment create 
microhabitats for various species. For example, the trees and bushes they 
14
push over provide shelter and food for smaller herbivores, insects, and birds 
(Pringle, 2008).
o Water holes created by elephants can become crucial habitats for 
amphibians, reptiles, and aquatic plants (Western, 1989).
• Climate Regulation:
o By maintaining open savanna landscapes, elephants help balance carbon 
storage. Grasslands, which they help sustain, are significant carbon sinks and 
play a role in sequestering atmospheric carbon dioxide (Bond et al., 2001).

Unit 2: Elephant Communication and Social Dynamics
Lesson 1: Communication Methods
Elephants exhibit complex communication methods that are integral to their social structure 
and survival. Their communication can be broadly categorized into vocal, non-vocal, seismic, 
and chemical signals, each serving different purposes in maintaining social bonds, 
coordinating movement, and avoiding danger.
Vocal Communication
• Rumbles:
The most common form of elephant vocalization is the low-frequency rumble, which 
can travel over long distances (up to several kilometers) through the ground and air. 
These rumbles are often used to maintain contact between individuals, especially in 
dense habitats where visibility is limited. Rumbles are also associated with greeting 
ceremonies, coordination of group movements, and mating calls. For instance, 
matriarchs often use specific rumbles to signal to the herd when to start moving 
(Poole & Granli, 2003).
• Trumpets:
Trumpeting is a more high-pitched sound that elephants produce when they are 
excited, frightened, or agitated. This sound can serve as a warning to other elephants 
about potential threats or as an expression of strong emotions such as joy during 
social interactions (Leong et al., 2003).
• Roars and Grunts:
These sounds are typically associated with aggression or distress. Roars can be heard 
during confrontations, while grunts are more commonly used in less intense 
situations, such as during foraging or when calves communicate with their mothers 
(Langbauer, 2000).
• Barking: Female elephants during estrus include vocalizations such as barking as part 
of the communication strategies to attract mates (Moss, 1983).
Non-Vocal Communication
• Body Language: 
Elephants use a variety of physical gestures to communicate. Ear flapping can 
indicate excitement or aggression, while trunk gestures (such as touching, caressing, 
or raising the trunk) are used in greetings or to reassure other members of the herd.
Tail position and movements also convey different emotions or intentions. A raised 
tail often signals alertness or agitation, while a drooping tail indicates relaxation 
(Poole & Granli, 2003).
16
• Touch: 
Touch is crucial for reinforcing social bonds within elephant groups. Elephants 
frequently use their trunks to caress, reassure, or play with each other. Mothers and 
calves engage in constant tactile communication to strengthen their bond (Moss, 
1988).
Seismic Communication
• Ground Vibrations:
Elephants have the remarkable ability to communicate through seismic signals, which 
involve the detection of vibrations transmitted through the ground. They produce 
these vibrations by stomping their feet or vocalizing low-frequency rumbles that 
create ground waves. These seismic signals can be detected by other elephants 
through their sensitive feet, which are specially adapted to perceive such vibrations.
Seismic communication is particularly useful for long-distance communication, 
especially in environments where visual or vocal signals may be less effective. For 
example, elephants can warn distant members of their herd about predators or 
coordinate group movements through these vibrations (O'Connell-Rodwell, 2007).
Chemical Communication
• Scent Marking:
Elephants use their acute sense of smell to communicate through chemical signals. 
They can identify individuals by their unique scent, which conveys information about 
identity, reproductive status, and even emotional state. Scent marking is particularly 
important during mating seasons, as bulls in musth release strong-smelling secretions 
to signal their reproductive status (Rasmussen, 1998).
• Urine and Feces:
Urine and feces also carry chemical cues that elephants use to communicate. These 
signals can convey information about health, reproductive status, and individual 
identity. This form of chemical communication helps maintain social hierarchies and 
facilitates mating behaviors (Moss, 1988).
Lesson 2: Social Structure and Family Bonds
Elephants are highly social animals that live in complex and structured societies. Their social 
structure is matriarchal, with tight-knit family groups forming the core of elephant society. 
These family units are essential for their survival, providing a network of support, protection, 
and knowledge transfer across generations.

Family Units
• Matriarchal Leadership: 
The core of an elephant social group is the family unit, typically consisting of related 
females and their offspring. The group is led by a matriarch, usually the oldest and 
most experienced female. The matriarch plays a crucial role in guiding the family,
making decisions about movement, foraging, and responding to threats. Her 
experience and knowledge, particularly about water sources and safe paths, are vital 
for the group’s survival, especially in challenging environments (Moss & Poole, 1983).
• Female-Centered Herds: 
Within the family unit, female elephants form strong, lifelong bonds. These bonds are 
reinforced through constant social interactions such as touching, vocalizing, and 
coordinated movement. These relationships are critical for maintaining group 
cohesion and ensuring the well-being of all members, particularly the young and 
vulnerable (Moss, 1988).
Extended Social Networks
• Clans and Bond Groups: 
Beyond the immediate family, elephants often form larger social networks called 
clans or bond groups. These groups consist of several related families that associate 
regularly. This larger network provides additional support and enhances the group's 
ability to defend against predators or navigate environmental challenges. Elephants 
within a clan maintain loose connections, often reuniting during periods of abundant 
resources (Douglas-Hamilton, 1972).
• Male Social Structure: 
While females remain with their birth group, male elephants typically leave the 
family unit upon reaching adolescence. Males either lead solitary lives or form loose 
associations with other males, known as bachelor groups. Despite their more solitary 
nature, males maintain social ties with their natal groups and interact with family 
units, especially during the mating season (Poole, 1994).
Social Bonds
• Allomothering: 
The upbringing of calves is a communal effort. Mothers, older siblings, and other 
related females, known as allomothers, all participate in caring for the young. This 
communal rearing ensures that calves learn essential survival skills, such as foraging 
techniques and social behaviors, from multiple members of the group. The matriarch 
and older females act as repositories of knowledge, passing down crucial information 
about the environment and social norms (Lee & Moss, 1986).
18
• Long-term Memory:
Elephants are known for their remarkable memory, which is essential for maintaining 
social bonds over long periods. They can remember individual members of their 
family and other groups for decades. This long-term memory plays a vital role in 
social cohesion and in reuniting with other groups or family members after long 
separations (Moss, 2001).
• Grieving and Loss:
Elephants display complex emotions and behaviors related to death and loss. When a 
family member dies, elephants often show signs of mourning, such as standing vigil 
over the body, touching the bones, and exhibiting distress behaviors. This deep 
emotional connection underscores the importance of family bonds in elephant 
society (Douglas-Hamilton et al., 2006).
Lesson 3: Reproductive Behavior and Mating Rituals
The reproductive behavior and mating rituals of African savanna elephants are complex 
processes influenced by social dynamics, environmental conditions, and individual status 
within the herd. Understanding these behaviors is key to conserving and managing elephant 
populations.
Sexual Maturity and Estrus
• Female elephants typically reach sexual maturity around 10 to 12 years of age, 
though they may not begin to breed until they are older, depending on 
environmental conditions and social factors. Males generally become sexually mature 
at a slightly older age, around 12 to 15 years, but they usually do not mate until they 
are in their mid-twenties, when they are more dominant and can compete 
successfully with older bulls (Moss, 1983).
• Female elephants experience a reproductive cycle known as estrus, which occurs 
every three to four months and lasts only a few days. During estrus, a female is 
receptive to mating, and her elevated hormone levels make her more attractive to 
males. Males detect females in estrus through chemical signals, particularly 
pheromones present in the female's urine (Poole, 1999).
Musth and Mating Behavior
• A distinctive feature of male elephant reproductive behavior is musth, a period of 
heightened sexual activity and aggression characterized by a significant increase in 
testosterone levels. During musth, males become more competitive and actively seek 
out estrous females. The musth condition is marked by physical changes, including 
the secretion of a strong-smelling substance from the temporal glands and frequent 
urination (Poole, 1987).
19
• When a female is in estrus, males in musth will often compete for the opportunity to 
mate with her. The competition can involve physical confrontations, where the 
stronger and more dominant male typically wins the right to mate. However, mating 
itself is a relatively quick process. The dominant bull will court the female, often 
following her closely and exhibiting behaviors such as trunk touching and 
vocalizations before mounting her (Moss, 1988).
Gestation and Birth
• After successful mating, the gestation period for an African savanna elephant is 
about 22 months, the longest of any land mammal. The long gestation allows the calf 
to develop fully before birth, which is essential for its survival. Typically, a single calf 
is born, although twins can occur rarely. Calving intervals are usually around four to 
five years, depending on environmental conditions and the availability of resources 
(Lee & Moss, 1986).
• Elephant calves are heavily dependent on their mothers and other females in the 
herd for care and protection. The mother is the primary caregiver, nursing the calf for 
up to two years, though calves may continue to suckle for comfort for several more 
years. The social structure of the herd supports the rearing of calves, with other 
females, often referred to as allomothers, assisting in the care of the young. This 
communal care system enhances the calf’s chances of survival and social integration 
(Moss, 1988).
Reproduction Strategies
• Female elephants employ various reproductive strategies, such as delayed 
reproduction during periods of environmental stress or in response to population 
density. These strategies help regulate the population and ensure that calves are 
born during favorable conditions, maximizing their chances of survival (Wittemyer, 
2001).
• The long gestation period and extended maternal care mean that elephant 
populations grow slowly, making them vulnerable to threats like poaching and 
habitat loss. Conservation efforts often focus on protecting female elephants and 
their calves to ensure the long-term viability of elephant populations (Blanc et al., 
2007).
Lesson 4: Role of Communication in Conflict Resolution
Mitigating Aggression
• Elephants employ a range of vocal signals to de-escalate potential conflicts. For 
instance, low-frequency rumbles and soft trumpeting can serve as calming signals, 
helping to diffuse tension before it escalates into physical aggression. Elephants often 
20
use these sounds to signal appeasement, especially in situations where younger or 
less dominant individuals are involved. Such vocalizations communicate a desire to 
avoid conflict, allowing the elephants to coexist peacefully (Poole & Granli, 2003).
• In addition to vocal communication, elephants use body language to mitigate 
aggression. Submissive postures, such as lowering the head or curling the trunk 
inward, can signal a willingness to back down from a confrontation. These non-verbal 
cues help prevent conflicts from escalating into physical altercations, which could 
result in injury to the individuals involved (Moss, 1988).
Reinforcing Hierarchies
• For male elephants, the phenomenon of musth plays a significant role in reinforcing 
social hierarchies. During musth, males exhibit heightened aggression and increased 
testosterone levels, marked by temporal gland secretions and frequent urination. 
Communication during musth involves both chemical signals and aggressive displays, 
which serve to establish and reinforce dominance within the male hierarchy. 
Dominant males in musth are often able to assert their position without resorting to 
physical conflict, as the chemical and visual signals are sufficient to deter less 
dominant males (Poole, 1987).
• Older, more experienced elephants often play a key role in maintaining social order 
within the herd. Their authority is communicated through subtle behavioral cues, 
such as leading movements, initiating group activities, and vocal affirmations. These 
behaviors reinforce their status and help maintain the hierarchical structure of the 
group, ensuring that social interactions remain stable and predictable (Moss, 1988).
Cooperative Behavior
• Communication is also essential in promoting cooperative behavior among 
elephants. Elephants coordinate their movements and activities through 
vocalizations, such as "let's go" rumbles, which signal the initiation of group travel or 
other collective actions. This coordination is particularly important when the group 
needs to move as a unit, such as during migration or when responding to threats 
(McComb et al., 2000).
• Elephants reinforce cooperative behavior through social bonding activities, such as 
mutual grooming and play. These interactions are accompanied by tactile 
communication, including trunk touches and gentle pushes, which help to strengthen 
social ties and encourage group cohesion. Strong social bonds within the herd 
facilitate cooperation, as individuals are more likely to work together when they have 
established positive relationships with each other (Lee & Moss, 1986).

Unit 3: Understanding Desert-Adapted Elephants
Lesson 1: Adaptations to Arid Environments
Desert-adapted elephants in Namibia are a remarkable example of nature's ability to thrive 
in extreme conditions. Found primarily in the Kunene Region, these elephants have 
developed unique adaptations that allow them to survive in the arid, semi-desert 
environment.
Physical Adaptations
1. Water Conservation:
o Hydration: Desert-adapted elephants have developed remarkable efficiency 
in water utilization. They can travel long distances between water sources and 
have a reduced dependency on daily drinking. Their kidneys are highly 
efficient in conserving water, producing concentrated urine to minimize water 
loss. Additionally, they have been observed digging wells in dry riverbeds to 
access subterranean water sources (Viljoen, 1989a).
2. Diet:
o Food consumption: These elephants have adapted their diet to the sparse 
and varied vegetation of the desert. They consume a wide range of plant 
species, including drought-resistant shrubs, succulents, and roots. Their ability 
to strip bark and dig for roots enables them to access nutrients that are not 
available to other herbivores. This diverse diet helps them survive periods of 
food scarcity (Leggett, 2006). The Ana trees (Faidherbia albida) and Camel 
thorn trees (Acacia erioloba) grow in the ephemeral rivers and are an 
essential food source for the desert elephants (EHRA, 2022a). Desert-adapted 
elephants also climb up hills and slopes in search for Commiphora bushes as 
food source (Ramey & Brown, 2019). 
3. Foot Structure:
o Larger Foot Pads: The feet of these elephants are well-adapted for walking 
long distances on sandy and rocky terrain. They have broader footpads that 
distribute their weight more evenly, reducing the risk of sinking into soft sand. 
This adaptation is crucial for their long migrations in search of food and water 
(Leggett, 2006).
4. Other unique traits:
o Brittle tusks: Tusks of desert-adpated elephants are often more brittle than 
that of other elephants in Namibia. This may be due to dehydration or a lack 
of minerals (EHRA, 2022b).
o Tusklessness: While some elephants may appear tuskless due to the 
aforementioned brittleness of the tusks, real tusklessness is an inherited trait 
22
found in some (exclusively female) elephants. In the Hoanib subpopulations, 
up to one-third of the cows are tuskless (Desert Elephant Conservation, n.d.).
Behavioral Adaptations
1. Extended Range and Migration Patterns:
• Desert elephants have significantly larger home ranges compared to other African 
elephants, sometimes covering areas over 12,000 square kilometers. This extensive 
range allows them to access dispersed water sources and food patches. They follow 
ancient migratory routes that have been passed down through generations, ensuring 
they can find resources even in the driest times (Viljoen, 1989b; Leggett, 2006).
2. Water Seeking Behavior:
• These elephants exhibit extraordinary abilities in locating water. They often dig wells 
in dry riverbeds to access underground water, providing essential hydration not only 
for themselves but also for other wildlife. This behavior demonstrates their deep 
knowledge of the landscape and water availability (Viljoen, 1989a). The digging of 
wells is seemingly also done to access cleaner water as desert-adapted elephants 
have been observed to dig for water close to already existing man-made boreholes 
that were contaminated with bacteria due to a plethora of species gathering and 
drinking water there (Ramey et al., 2013).
3. Efficient Foraging:
• These elephants have adapted to forage during cooler parts of the day, such as early 
morning and late afternoon, to minimize water loss and heat stress. During the 
hottest parts of the day, they rest in shaded areas to conserve energy and reduce the 
need for water (Viljoen, 1988).
4. Calm Temperament:
• Desert elephants exhibit a notably calmer temperament, likely an adaptation to 
conserve energy. They move more slowly and are less likely to engage in aggressive 
behavior, which helps reduce unnecessary energy expenditure and water loss 
through exertion (Leggett, 2006).

Lesson 3: Conservation Challenges and Efforts
• Challenges:
o Habitat fragmentation: Habitat fragmentation in Namibia, caused by human 
activities such as farming, urbanization, and road construction, is a significant 
challenge for the conservation of desert-adapted elephants. These activities 
break up the elephants' natural habitats, limiting their movement and access 
to essential resources like water and forage. Fragmentation can also isolate 
elephant populations, which reduces genetic diversity and makes it more 
difficult for these populations to adapt to environmental changes. As 
elephants are forced into smaller areas, conflicts with humans over resources 
increase, threatening both elephant survival and local livelihoods (Save the 
Elephants, n.d.).
24
o Climate change: Climate change threatens desert-adapted elephants by 
intensifying water scarcity and reducing vegetation availability, which are vital 
for their survival. Increased droughts force elephants to travel further for 
water, heightening the risk of human-elephant conflicts as they encroach on 
human settlements. This combination of environmental stressors makes it 
harder for these elephants to survive in an already harsh desert environment
(WWF, 2014).
o Human-elephant conflict: The desert-adapted elephants, originally migrating 
from the northern regions where they coexisted peacefully with the nomadic 
Himba people, have expanded their range as far south as the Erongo Region 
and eastward onto commercial farmland. However, many Damara and Herero 
communities, who have recently settled in the arid homelands of northwest 
Namibia, are unfamiliar with elephants. As a result, they often experience fear 
and frustration when elephants approach their water reservoirs near 
homesteads (EHRA, 2022d).
o High calf mortality rate: Desert elephants in Namibia are facing high calf 
mortality which means that populations are especially vulnerable (EHRA, 
2022b). Between the years 2014 and 2018, nine out of 14 calves died after 
birth from unknown causes (EHRA, 2022e). 
o Low number of breeding-age bulls: For the subpopulations of the Hoarusib, 
Hoanib, and Uniab river drainages, the low number of breeding-age bulls is of 
concern: there is a risk of inbreeding with only few male individuals left, and 
the populations face extinction if the males left are killed or die prematurely 
of other causes (Brown & Ramey, 2024).
• Conservation Efforts:
o Desert-adapted elephants are essential for their ecosystem as they provide 
water for other animals by digging wells and help with seed dispersal through 
their dieting and migrating behavior (EHRA, 2022e).
o If desert elephants were to go extinct, it is unlikely that other savanna 
elephants would be able to naturally replace them, as they lack the 
specialized behaviors required for survival in desert environments (EHRA, 
2022e).
o Initiatives include the establishment of protected areas, implementing 
community-based conservation programs, and conducting education 
campaigns to raise awareness about the importance of these unique 
elephants (EHRA, 2022e).

Lesson 1: Indigenous Practices for Elephant Conservation
Traditional Methods of Living Harmoniously with Elephants
1. Land Use and Resource Management:
o Seasonal Land Use: Indigenous groups, such as the Himba, plan their 
agricultural and pastoral activities according to the seasonal movements of 
elephants. By avoiding areas known to be frequented by elephants during 
certain times of the year, they minimize the risk of encounters and crop 
damage (Sullivan, 1999).
o Water Source Management: The use of traditional wells and the knowledge 
of natural water holes, which elephants frequently use, helps in managing 
water resources sustainably (Hoole & Berkes, 2010). To reduce competition 
and direct conflict over water, some communities create separate water 
points for elephants away from human settlements. This ensures that 
elephants have access to water without encroaching on village supplies 
(Hoare, 1999).
2. Natural Fencing:
o Thorn Bush Barriers: Many communities use thorny branches from native 
plants to create natural barriers around their fields. These "living fences" are 
effective in deterring elephants due to the discomfort they cause when 
elephants try to breach them. The Acacia species, known for their dense 
thorns, are commonly used for this purpose (Hoole & Berkes, 2010).
Indigenous Knowledge of Elephant Behavior and Migration Routes
Indigenous communities in Namibia, such as the Himba, San, and other local groups, have 
accumulated extensive knowledge about elephant behavior and migration routes through 
generations of close interaction with their natural environment. This knowledge is deeply 
rooted in their cultural practices, oral traditions, and daily observations, allowing them to 
coexist with elephants and manage potential conflicts effectively.
1. Understanding of Elephant Behavior:
o Behavioral Cues: Indigenous people have developed a keen sense of 
interpreting various behavioral cues of elephants. They can predict the 
presence and movement of elephants based on subtle signs such as changes 
in the environment, the behavior of other wildlife, and specific elephant 
sounds. For instance, a change in bird calls or the sudden quietness of the 
26
forest can signal the approach of elephants (O’Connell-Rodwell et al., 2000).
o Seasonal Patterns: Knowledge about the seasonal behavior of elephants is 
also crucial. Indigenous communities are aware of the times of the year when 
elephants are likely to move in search of water and food. This understanding 
allows them to anticipate and prepare for elephant movements, ensuring that 
human activities do not interfere with elephant routes and needs (Sullivan, 
1999).
2. Migration Routes:
o Historical Routes: Indigenous communities have mapped out historical 
elephant migration routes that have been used for centuries. These routes 
are typically passed down through generations via oral traditions and stories. 
The San people, for instance, have extensive knowledge of ancient elephant 
paths that crisscross their territories, guiding elephants between water 
sources and feeding areas (O’Connell-Rodwell et al., 2000).
o Natural Markers: Elephants often follow specific natural markers such as 
riverbeds, mountain ranges, and vegetation patterns. Indigenous knowledge 
includes an understanding of these markers, helping to identify and predict 
elephant routes. This insight is critical for avoiding conflicts, especially during 
agricultural activities or settlement expansions (Hoole & Berkes, 2010).
o Water Sources: The location and availability of water sources are key 
determinants of elephant migration. Indigenous people know the seasonal 
availability of water in various locations and how this influences elephant 
movements. For instance, during dry seasons, elephants tend to congregate 
near reliable water sources known to local communities (Sullivan, 1999).
Cultural Taboos and Rituals Related to Elephants
1. Cultural Taboos:
o Harming Elephants: Among several indigenous groups, there is a strong taboo 
against harming or killing elephants. It is believed that such actions can bring 
misfortune or bad luck to the individual and their community. For example, 
the Himba people view elephants as sacred animals and believe that harming 
them can lead to severe consequences, including drought or famine. The San 
people, also known as Bushmen, believe that elephants have a spiritual 
connection to their ancestors. This belief system creates a profound respect 
for elephants and a reluctance to interfere with their natural behaviors or 
habitats (O'Connell-Rodwell et al., 2000; Sullivan, 1999).
2. Rituals and Symbolic Practices:
o Elephant Ceremonies: Certain rituals are performed to honor elephants and 
seek their protection. These ceremonies often involve offerings, prayers, and 
dances. For example, the Herero people conduct rituals that include chanting 
and dancing to appease the spirits of elephants and ask for their blessing, 
27
particularly during times of hardship or drought (Suzman, 2002).
o Storytelling and Oral Traditions: Storytelling is a crucial aspect of indigenous 
cultures in Namibia. Elders pass down stories about elephants, which often 
contain moral lessons about respect and coexistence. These stories serve to 
educate younger generations about the importance of elephants and the 
need to protect them. For instance, tales of elephants helping humans or 
punishing those who disrespect them are common among the Himba and

o Conflict Resolution Rituals: In cases of human-elephant conflict, some 
communities perform specific rituals to restore harmony. These might involve 
cleansing ceremonies to purify areas where conflicts have occurred, or the 
symbolic reconciliation between humans and elephants through offerings and 
prayers. Such practices emphasize the need for balance and mutual respect 
between species (O'Connell-Rodwell et al., 2000).

In Namibia, the integration of traditional practices and indigenous knowledge with modern 
conservation efforts has become a pivotal strategy in the sustainable management of

o Educational Programs: Conservation organizations collaborate with local

pass down indigenous knowledge to younger generations while promoting

o Local communities utilize traditional early warning systems to prevent human elephant conflicts. For instance, the Himba and other groups interpret

Lesson 2: Dos and Don’ts when Encountering Elephants
Encountering elephants in the wild is a memorable experience, but it requires caution and 
respect for their space and behavior. By following these do's and don'ts, you can ensure 
both your safety and the well-being of these magnificent creatures. Always prioritize 
observing elephants from a distance and avoid actions that could disturb or threaten them.
Do
1. Stay Quiet and Calm:
If you encounter an elephant in the wild, stay calm and avoid making sudden 
movements or loud noises. Elephants are sensitive to disturbances and can be 
startled easily (Poole & Granli, 2008).
2. Keep a Safe Distance:
Always maintain a safe distance from elephants. A minimum distance of 100 meters 
is recommended to avoid disturbing them and to ensure your safety (Journal of 
African Elephants, 2024).
3. Be Aware of Body Language:
Pay attention to the elephants' body language. If they appear agitated (flapping ears, 
29
trumpeting, mock charging), slowly and calmly retreat (Moss, 2001).
4. Respect Their Space:
Always give elephants the right of way. If an elephant is on the path, wait for it to 
move rather than trying to pass by. Use binoculars to observe elephants from a safe 
distance without approaching them (Journal of African Elephants, 2024).
5. Follow the Guide's Instructions:
If you are on a guided safari, always follow the guide’s instructions. They are trained 
to read elephant behavior and know how to react in different situations.
Don't
1. Don't Approach:
Never approach an elephant, especially not on foot. Elephants can become 
aggressive if they feel threatened (Moss, 2001).
2. Don't Feed Elephants:
Feeding elephants can make them associate humans and vehicles with food, leading 
to dangerous situations and increased human-elephant conflict (LNT, 2024).
3. Don't Use Flash Photography:
Avoid using flash photography as it can startle and agitate elephants (Poole & Granli, 
2008).
4. Don't Get Out of Your Vehicle:
If you are in a vehicle, remain inside. Getting out can make you more vulnerable and 
increase the risk of a dangerous encounter. In areas with known elephant presence, 
keep vehicle windows closed to prevent elephants from reaching inside (Journal of 
African Elephants, 2024).
5. Don't Attempt to Drive Past an Elephant:
When encountering elephants while driving, turn off your engine to reduce noise and 
stay inside the vehicle (Journal of African Elephants, 2024).
6. Don't Make Loud Noises:
Refrain from honking, shouting, or making other loud noises. This can stress the 
elephants and provoke an aggressive response (Journal of African Elephants, 2024).
7. Do Not Block Their Path:
Never position yourself between an elephant and its herd or water source, especially 
not between a cow and her calf. This can make the elephant feel threatened and lead 
to a charge (Journal of African Elephants, 2024).
8. Do Not Ignore Warning Signs:
Disregarding signs of agitation such as ear flapping, trunk swinging, or mock charging 
30
can result in dangerous encounters. Always back off if you notice these behaviors 
(Moss, 2001).

Mitigating human-elephant conflict in Namibia relies heavily on integrating indigenous 
strategies that have been used for generations with modern conservation efforts. These 
methods are sustainable and respect both the local communities' ways of life and the 
elephants' natural behaviors.

Crop Protection Techniques
1. Natural Fencing:
o Thorn Bush Barriers: Indigenous communities use thorn bushes to create 
natural fences around their fields. These barriers are effective in deterring 
elephants from entering cultivated areas (Hoare, 1999).
o Living Fences: Planting specific types of trees or shrubs that elephants find 
unappealing around fields can also serve as a natural deterrent (Naughton Treves, 1997).
2. Diversified Cropping:
o Mixed Cropping: Planting crops that elephants do not favor, such as chili 
peppers, among other crops can reduce the attractiveness of fields to 
elephants. This technique not only protects the primary crops but also 
provides additional sources of income for farmers (Osborn & Parker, 2003).
o Buffer Zones: Creating buffer zones with unpalatable crops around the main 
crops can act as a deterrent. These zones can include crops like ginger, garlic, 
or lemongrass, which are less attractive to elephants (Hoare, 1999).
3. Beehive Fences: Utilizing beehive fences is an innovative method where beehives are 
hung along wires around crop fields. Elephants avoid these areas because they are 
wary of bees. The presence of bees not only deters elephants but also provides 
additional income through honey production (King et al., 2011; King et al., 2017).
Elephant Deterrent Methods
1. Noise and Light:
o Drums and Bells: Indigenous communities often use drums, bells, and other 
noisy instruments to scare elephants away from their settlements. The 
sudden noise startles the elephants and encourages them to move away

o Fires and Torches: Lighting fires and producing smoke can create a visual

discourages elephants from entering agricultural areas (Osborn & Parker,

2. Watchtowers:
o Observation Posts: Building elevated watchtowers around the periphery of 
fields allows community members to monitor elephant movements and 
respond quickly to potential threats. This method helps in early detection and 
timely intervention (Hoare, 1999).

Lesson 4: Orphan Care and Conservation
Elephant orphan care in Namibia is a vital component of conservation efforts, ensuring that 
young elephants who have lost their families due to poaching, human-wildlife conflict, or 
natural causes receive the necessary care and support for survival.
Elephant Orphan Care
In Namibia, as in other parts of Africa, orphaned elephants are a result of various human wildlife conflicts, including poaching, habitat destruction, and occasionally natural causes 
like drought or disease. Orphan care programs play a crucial role in providing these young 
elephants with a chance to survive and thrive.
1. Rescue and Rehabilitation:
o Rescue Operations: When a calf is found orphaned, MEFT need to be 
contacted since only trained teams are allowed to conduct rescue and 
rehabilitation missions (MEFT, 2021). These operations involve tracking, 
tranquilizing (if necessary), and transporting the calf to a rehabilitation center
(Hoole & Berkes, 2010).
o Immediate Care: Upon arrival at the rehabilitation center, orphaned 
elephants receive immediate medical attention. This includes treating any 
injuries, dehydration, or malnourishment. Veterinary staff closely monitor the 
health and progress of the calves during their initial days in the center 
(Douglas-Hamilton et al., 2005).
2. Nurturing and Socialization:
o Nutrition and Care: Orphaned elephants receive a diet that mimics their 
natural intake, including specialized milk formulas and vegetation. Caregivers, 
who often form close bonds with the elephants, provide the comfort and 
security typically offered by the mothers (IFAW, n.d.).
o Socialization with Other Elephants: Rehabilitation centers facilitate 
interactions among orphaned calves to help them develop social skills and 
establish a support network within a pseudo-herd structure. This helps 
replicate the social dynamics they would experience in the wild (Poole & 
Granli, 2011).
3. Reintegration into Wild Herds:
34
o Gradual Reintroduction: Orphaned elephants are initially exposed to 
controlled environments where they can interact with wild herds under 
supervision. This helps them acclimate to natural surroundings and potential 
herd members (Poole & Granli, 2011).
o Monitoring and Support: Reintegrated elephants are monitored using tracking 
devices to ensure their adaptation to the wild. Conservation teams provide 
ongoing support as needed, intervening only in cases of significant challenges 
(O'Connell-Rodwell et al., 2000).
o Establishing Bonds with Wild Herds: The social bonds formed during 
rehabilitation aid in the acceptance of orphaned elephants by wild herds. 
These bonds replicate the family structures found in natural herds, facilitating 
successful reintegration (IFAW, n.d.)
Importance of Preserving Elephant Family Structures
1. Social Bonds and Learning:
o Role of the Matriarch: The matriarch's knowledge about migration routes, 
water sources, and safe havens is passed down through generations, ensuring 
the herd's survival. Studies have shown that herds with experienced 
matriarchs fare better during environmental challenges (McComb et al., 
2001). The matriarch also plays a pivotal role in conflict resolution within the 
herd, helping to maintain social harmony (Moss & Poole, 1983).
o Learning and Cultural Transmission: Young elephants learn essential survival 
skills from older family members. These skills include recognizing threats, 
finding food and water, and social behaviors necessary for integration into the 
herd. This cultural transmission is vital for the continuity of the herd's 
knowledge base (Poole & Granli, 2008).
2. Emotional Well-being:
o Emotional Bonds: Elephants form deep emotional bonds with family 
members. These bonds are evident in behaviors such as greeting ceremonies, 
communal care of calves, and mourning the death of a herd member. 
Disrupting these bonds can lead to severe psychological stress and behavioral 
changes (Bradshaw et al., 2005).
o Impact of Family Disruption: Poaching and human-wildlife conflict can lead to 
the loss of family members, causing trauma and long-term emotional distress. 
Orphaned calves, in particular, suffer from the absence of their mothers, 
leading to decreased chances of survival and social integration (Bradshaw et 
al., 2005; Douglas-Hamilton et al., 2005).
3. Ecological Impact:
35
o Keystone Species: Elephants are considered a keystone species due to their 
ability to shape their environment. They help maintain the savanna and forest 
ecosystems by controlling vegetation growth, dispersing seeds, and creating 
water holes used by other wildlife. The loss of elephant populations can lead 
to significant ecological changes, affecting biodiversity and ecosystem health 
(Van Aarde & Jackson, 2007).
o Habitat Modification: Elephants’ foraging behavior, which involves knocking 
down trees and clearing vegetation, creates open areas that benefit other 
species. This behavior promotes a heterogeneous environment that supports 
a diverse array of plant and animal life (Pringle, 2008).
4. Cultural and Ethical Considerations:
o Cultural Significance: In Namibia, elephants are revered in various cultural 
traditions and folklore. For example, the Himba people consider elephants to 
be sacred and view them as symbols of strength and wisdom. Elephants are 
often featured in local myths and rituals, highlighting their importance in 
cultural identity (Sullivan, 1998).
o Ethical Responsibility: Preserving elephant family structures is not only an 
ecological and emotional necessity but also an ethical responsibility. Ensuring 
the well-being of these sentient beings, who exhibit profound emotional 
complexity, aligns with broader conservation ethics and the principle of 
respecting animal rights (Bekoff, 2007).
Adoption and Community Involvement

Lesson 1: Conservation Challenges and Threats
Human-Elephant Conflict Hotspots
1. Farmland and Agricultural Areas:
o Elephants in Namibia often come into conflict with local communities over 
access to crops and water sources. Agricultural areas near elephant habitats 
are frequent conflict zones where elephants may raid crops, leading to 
economic losses for farmers (O’Connell-Rodwell et al., 2000).
2. Water Points and Human Settlements:
o Competition for water resources can escalate conflict between elephants and 
humans, especially during drought periods when water sources become 
scarce. Elephants may damage infrastructure or intimidate communities near 
water points (Hoare, 1999).
3. Infrastructure Development:
o Roads, railways, and other infrastructure projects can fragment elephant 
habitats, disrupt migratory routes, and increase the risk of human-elephant 
conflict. Encounters with vehicles pose additional threats to both elephants 
and human safety (Hoare, 2015).
Poaching Threats and Illegal Wildlife Trade
1. Ivory Poaching:
o Namibia, like many other African countries, has experienced a resurgence in 
elephant poaching driven by the demand for ivory in international markets. 
Poaching not only reduces elephant populations but also destabilizes their 
social structures, as poachers often target older elephants with larger tusks 
(UNODC, 2020).
2. Bushmeat Trade:
o The bushmeat trade, while often associated with smaller wildlife, also impacts 
elephants in Namibia. In some cases, elephants are killed for their meat, 
which is sold or consumed locally. This practice can be particularly prevalent 
in areas where communities face food insecurity or lack alternative protein 
sources. The bushmeat trade is exacerbated by the remote nature of some 
regions, where law enforcement is limited, and poaching can go unchecked 
(Lindsey et al., 2013).
38
3. Combined Threats:
o The combined pressures of the ivory trade and bushmeat trade create a dual 
threat to elephant populations. The bushmeat trade often goes hand in hand 
with ivory poaching, as poachers who kill elephants for their tusks may also 
sell the meat. This increases the economic incentives for poaching and further 
endangers elephant populations, particularly in regions where enforcement 
of wildlife laws is weak (Milner-Gulland & Bennett, 2003).
Habitat Loss and Fragmentation
1. Protected Areas:
o While Namibia has established several protected areas to conserve elephant 
habitats, the connectivity between these areas is often limited, further 
exacerbating habitat fragmentation. The lack of wildlife corridors hinders the 
natural migration patterns of elephants, leading to conflicts as elephants 
move into human-dominated landscapes (Harris et al., 2009).
2. Land Conversion:
o Conversion of natural habitats into agricultural land, mining areas, or urban 
developments further diminishes suitable elephant habitat. Fragmented 
landscapes isolate elephant populations, reducing genetic diversity and 
increasing vulnerability to environmental changes (Bennett, 1999).
Climate Change Impacts on Elephant Populations
1. Shifts in Vegetation:
o Climate change can lead to shifts in vegetation patterns, with some areas 
becoming less suitable for elephants. This could result in elephants migrating 
to new areas, potentially leading to further conflicts with human communities 
and other wildlife (WWF, 2014).
2. Water Scarcity:
o As a semi-arid country, Namibia is particularly vulnerable to climate change 
impacts such as prolonged droughts. Water scarcity not only affects the 
availability of drinking water for elephants but also the growth of vegetation 
they rely on for food. This forces elephants to travel greater distances in 
search of water and food (Ripple et al., 2015).
3. Human-Wildlife Conflict Intensification:
o These factors combined may exacerbate human-wildlife conflict as elephants 
increasingly encroach on agricultural lands or human settlements in search of 
food and water. This escalation strains community tolerance and conservation 
efforts (Ripple et al. 2015).

Conservation Efforts and Strategies
Despite these challenges, conservation efforts in Namibia focus on several strategies to 
mitigate threats and protect elephant populations:
• Community-Based Conservation: 
Namibia has pioneered community-based conservation through the establishment of 
communal conservancies. These conservancies allow local communities to manage 
and benefit from wildlife, including elephants, creating incentives for conservation. 
Through these conservancies, communities gain economic benefits from tourism and 
sustainable use of wildlife, which helps reduce poaching and human-elephant conflict 
(Naidoo et al., 2011; MEFT, 2019).
• Anti-Poaching Measures: 
Anti-poaching measures in Namibia include the deployment of wildlife rangers and 
the use of advanced technologies like aerial surveillance and GPS tracking. These 
efforts are aimed at deterring poaching activities, particularly in regions like the 
Zambezi and Kavango, where poaching pressures are higher (WWF, 2018a).
• Habitat Protection and Restoration: 
Namibia has designated several protected areas and wildlife corridors to ensure that 
elephants have access to large, connected habitats. Efforts are also being made to 
restore degraded habitats, particularly in areas affected by human activities, to 
support the ecological needs of elephants and reduce human-elephant conflicts 
(Bennett, 1999; Harris et al., 2009).
• Education and Awareness: 
Education and awareness campaigns are crucial for promoting elephant 
conservation. These programs target local communities, schools, and the general 
public to raise awareness about the importance of elephants, the threats they face, 
and ways to coexist with them. Such efforts help build local support for conservation 
initiatives and reduce negative interactions between humans and elephants (WWF, 
2018b; MEFT, 2019).
• Climate Resilience: 
As climate change poses long-term challenges for elephant conservation, efforts are 
being made to enhance the resilience of elephant habitats. This includes the 
management of water resources, the preservation of vegetation, and the creation of 
drought-resistant habitats. By making ecosystems more resilient to climate change, 
these efforts help ensure that elephants have the resources they need to survive in a 
changing environment (Ripple et al., 2015; WWF, 2014).

Lesson 2: Conservation Policies and Initiatives
Government Regulations and Protected Areas
Namibia's conservation framework is grounded in robust government regulations that are 
designed to protect wildlife, including elephants. The Nature Conservation Ordinance of 
1975, and subsequent amendments, provide the legal basis for wildlife protection in the 
country. This ordinance, among other things, regulates hunting and the trade of elephant 
products, ensuring that these activities do not threaten elephant populations. Furthermore, 
Namibia has established extensive protected areas, such as Etosha National Park, 
Bwabwata National Park, and the Zambezi Region, which are critical habitats for elephants. 
These protected areas are vital in maintaining healthy elephant populations and minimizing 
human-elephant conflict by preserving large tracts of undisturbed land (MEFT, 2019; Naidoo 
et al., 2011).
Role of NGOs and Community-Based Organizations
Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) and Community-Based Organizations (CBOs) are 
essential players in Namibia's elephant conservation efforts. Organizations and local NGOs 
collaborate with local communities to implement conservation initiatives. These efforts are 
particularly evident in the communal conservancy program, where communities manage 
wildlife resources and derive economic benefits from them. This model not only promotes 
conservation but also ensures that local people are directly involved and benefit from 
sustainable wildlife management. The success of this approach is evident in the widespread 
establishment of communal conservancies across Namibia, which now cover nearly 20% of 
the country (Naidoo et al., 2011; IRDNC, 2021).
Conservation Strategies for Mitigating Human-Elephant Conflict
Addressing human-elephant conflict is a critical aspect of Namibia’s conservation strategy. 
The country has implemented various measures, such as early warning systems, which help 
communities anticipate and prevent elephant raids. Additionally, physical barriers, like 
fences, are employed to protect crops and property from elephant incursions. Namibia also 
promotes alternative farming techniques that are less attractive to elephants, thereby 
reducing the likelihood of conflicts. Community engagement is central to these efforts, with 
programs designed to educate locals on how to coexist with elephants and provide 
compensation for any losses incurred due to elephant activities. These strategies help to 
mitigate the economic impact on communities while fostering local support for elephant 
conservation (MEFT, 2019).
Sustainable Tourism Practices for Elephant Conservation
Sustainable tourism is a cornerstone of Namibia’s conservation strategy, particularly in 
communal conservancies. Eco-tourism initiatives provide a significant source of income for 
local communities while promoting the conservation of elephants. Guidelines have been 
established to ensure that tourism activities do not negatively impact elephant populations. 
These guidelines include maintaining a safe distance from elephants, minimizing noise, and 
educating tourists on the importance of conservation. By adhering to these practices, 
tourism in Namibia not only supports conservation efforts financially but also raises 
awareness of the challenges faced by elephants and the importance of their preservation 
(IRDNC, 2021).
